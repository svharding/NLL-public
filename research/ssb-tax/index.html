The Newfoundland and Labrador sugar-sweetened beverage tax research program

Prices, purchasing, perceptions and equity: evaluating a provincial beverage policy.

Before the taxTax evaluationPricing & marketingPerceptions & normsPutting it together

Newfoundland and Labrador made history in 2022 as the first province in Canada to tax sugar-sweetened beverages (SSBs). Our lab, led by Dr. Scott Harding together with Dr. Rachel Prowse and a large team of students and research assistants, has spent the past four years documenting what happened before, during, and after that policy took effect. Together, six pieces of work tell the story: a baseline study of pre-tax drinking habits, a full evaluation of the tax's first-year impact, two closer looks at online and in-store pricing/marketing mechanics, a boil-water-advisory and attitudes study, and a manuscript now under revision on beverage perceptions and social norms. Below is a walk-through of what each piece found and how they connect.

Setting the stage: what were people drinking before the tax?

(Zaltz et al., 2025, BMC Public Health)

Before you can evaluate a policy, you need to know what things looked like beforehand. In August 2022, just weeks before the tax took effect, our team surveyed 1,233 adults across NL about their beverage habits. The picture wasn't encouraging: 57% drank a taxable SSB (regular pop, sports drinks, energy drinks, and similar) every week, and regular pop alone accounted for an average of 2.3 litres per week among those who drank it.

More importantly, this baseline study revealed clear social patterning. People with less education, lower income, or who experienced food insecurity were both more likely to drink SSBs and less likely to drink unsweetened beverages like water. This wasn't a new finding in the broader literature, but it hadn't been documented in NL specifically, and it mattered a lot for interpreting what came next: if healthier alternatives remain unaffordable, or if a tax disproportionately affects people who are already struggling, that's a serious equity concern worth watching closely.

Did the tax actually work? The full evaluation

(Prowse & Harding, "Evaluating Canada's First Sugar-Sweetened Beverage Tax in Newfoundland and Labrador," June 2025, commissioned by Heart & Stroke)

This is the flagship report, drawing on years of data collection: consumer surveys before and after the tax (over 3,200 people combined), nearly 400 in-person store audits, over 100 online store audits, and two years of retail sales data covering more than 300 million litres of beverages across NL and the Maritime provinces (used as a comparison region with no SSB tax).

The headline findings:

  • Prices barely moved where consumers could see them. Posted shelf prices for taxable SSBs didn't change relative to non-taxed drinks. That's because the tax was rarely visible: fewer than one in four taxed beverages had the $0.20/litre charge shown on the shelf tag. Most stores buried it as a hidden fee at checkout, similar to a bottle deposit. When the tax was shown, it was almost always at the correct rate, but invisibility undermines the whole point of a "sin tax" — if shoppers can't see the extra cost when they're deciding what to buy, the tax can't nudge their behaviour at the moment that matters.
  • But behaviour changed anyway. Per-capita sales of taxable SSBs fell 12% in NL, compared to a smaller 7% decline in the untaxed Maritime provinces over the same period — consistent with a possible tax-related effect beyond general trends. Regular pop, the top-selling taxed beverage, saw sales drop 13% in NL versus 8% in the Maritimes. Survey data backed this up: the odds of drinking any taxable SSB in a given week fell 24% after the tax, once other factors were controlled for.
  • But people didn't cut sugar out entirely — they moved it around. Here's the catch: the odds of drinking non-taxable sugary drinks (flavoured milk, specialty coffees, smoothies — categories the tax doesn't cover) rose 52%. Diet beverage consumption also increased. This pattern is consistent with substitution toward exempt drinks, although these population-level comparisons cannot establish individual switching or the net change in total sugar intake.
  • Equity concerns emerged. Contrary to what's usually hoped for with these taxes, it was food-secure and higher-income households that cut back the most. Lower-income and food-insecure households showed little to no significant change — meaning the people the tax was arguably most meant to help were the least affected by it.
  • Boil water advisories didn't seem to push people toward SSBs. The team also checked whether NL's chronic water-quality issues (some communities have lived under boil-water advisories for decades) were driving people away from tap water and toward sugary alternatives. Interestingly, neither actual nor perceived boil-water advisories predicted SSB intake — though negative attitudes toward tap water in general did predict lower water consumption.

The report's bottom line: the $0.20/litre tax (about an 8.5% average price increase) is well below the 20% increase the WHO recommends for meaningful behaviour change, and its patchy visibility at the point of sale limited its power. The recommendations were blunt — keep the tax, raise it, close the loopholes (non-taxed sugary drinks, made-to-order beverages, chocolate milk), require visible pricing, and reinvest the revenue in equity-focused programs. Notably, the Government of NL announced its intention to repeal the tax around the same time this report was finalized, which the authors argue would forfeit whatever health gains had started to take hold.

Did prices actually move? Two closer looks at pricing and marketing

Two companion papers dig into the mechanics behind the flagship report's headline finding that the tax wasn't visible where it mattered — the online grocery price study and the in-store pricing/marketing audit.

Online prices and sales across Canada

(Prowse et al., 2025, Health Promotion International)

This study compared online grocery prices and retail sales data for NL against several non-tax comparison regions (Nova Scotia, New Brunswick, Manitoba, and Yukon). Using a controlled interrupted time series on prices from 15 store websites (53 indicator beverages, over 13,000 price observations), the team found no significant change in the intercept or slope of taxable SSB prices in NL relative to the comparison regions in the three months around the tax's introduction — mirroring the flagship report's in-store finding that the tax simply wasn't showing up on posted prices.

Despite that, the separate sales-data analysis (over 300 million litres of beverages sold across NL and the Maritimes) showed real behaviour change: per-capita taxable SSB sales fell 11.6% in NL versus 6.7% in the Maritimes, with regular soft drink sales dropping 13.0% in NL versus 7.6% in the Maritimes. Diet beverage and water sales rose more in NL than in the Maritimes as well. The authors describe this as a possible "signalling effect" — people cut back even without seeing a price change, perhaps simply because they knew a tax existed — while cautioning that the discrepancy between the price-side and sales-side findings is hard to fully untangle from broader market trends.

In-store pricing and marketing audits

(Dooley et al., accepted, Public Health Nutrition)

This study went inside 80 physical stores (grocery, convenience, drug, and dollar stores) across NL, auditing posted shelf prices and marketing (discounts, product placement, promotional signage) before and after the tax, across nearly 26,000 beverage observations. Like the online-price study, it found no evidence that posted shelf prices for taxable SSBs changed relative to non-taxable beverages — reinforcing that the tax wasn't being communicated to shoppers at the moment of decision-making, regardless of whether they were shopping online or in person.

Where this study adds something new is in marketing behaviour: the proportion of taxable SSBs on discount actually rose slightly after the tax (from 38.0% to 40.5% of taxable SSBs), while non-taxable beverages saw no significant change — with the increase concentrated in energy drinks and iced teas specifically. Depth of discount also increased for both taxable and non-taxable beverages post-tax, and there was a non-significant uptick in the number of in-store promotions and product placements for taxable SSBs. The authors frame this cautiously: without a no-tax comparison region for the in-store data, they can't say for certain these shifts are attributable to the tax rather than ordinary market variation, but they note the pattern doesn't look like industry actively fighting the tax (as seen in some other jurisdictions) so much as retailers simply not bothering to surface it, consistent with a regulatory gap that leaves display of the tax entirely up to individual stores.

Why do people drink what they drink? Perceptions and social norms

(manuscript, submitted August 2026)

The third piece of this puzzle asks a more psychological question: beyond price, what makes someone reach for a soda instead of water? This study surveyed the same post-tax cohort (2,008 adults) about how healthy or unhealthy they perceive different beverages to be, whether they'd approve of a child drinking them, and whether they feel social pressure — from friends, family, or their own sense of what's "right" — to avoid sugary drinks.

Some notable findings:

  • Regular and diet soda were seen as the least healthy options by a wide margin (only 9.5% and 14.6% of people rated them as neutral-to-healthy), while water and milk were seen as nearly universally healthy (over 92%).
  • Holding a positive or neutral view of a beverage's healthiness strongly predicted actually drinking it — for every single beverage studied. The effect was strongest for diet soda (people who saw it as "not unhealthy" were over three times more likely to drink it).
  • Interestingly, perceptions "clustered": people who saw orange juice favourably were also more likely to drink regular soda, and vice versa, suggesting these choices travel together as a broader dietary pattern rather than being made independently.
  • Social norms mattered too, but in a surprising, beverage-specific way. Believing that people around you disapprove of sugary drinks was linked to lower odds of drinking regular soda — but higher odds of drinking diet soda. This hints that some people use diet soda as a substitute they feel is more socially acceptable when they're trying to cut back.

This work matters because it points to a lever beyond taxation: public health messaging and social norm campaigns that shift how people perceive beverages could complement price-based policy, especially since the tax evaluation found so many people substituting toward other sugary options rather than cutting back altogether.

Putting it together

Read as a set, these six pieces trace a full policy life cycle: what things looked like before the intervention, what changed (and didn't) after it — both in dollars-and-cents terms and in the fine grain of pricing and marketing tactics — why people made the choices they did (including how water quality and trust shape those choices), and what should happen next. The consistent thread across the pricing-focused papers is remarkably stable: whether measured online, in physical stores, or in the flagship report, the tax was rarely visible on posted price tags, yet sales still shifted in the expected direction — suggesting a "signalling effect" independent of visible cost, layered on top of loopholes people used to move their sugar consumption elsewhere. Real, durable change looks like it will require a bigger price signal, a wider net that catches the sugary drinks currently exempted, mandated visible pricing at the point of decision-making, and complementary efforts — from water infrastructure and trust-building to shifting social norms — that work alongside the tax rather than assuming price alone will do the job.
