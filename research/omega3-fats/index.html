DIETARY FATS

Does the form of omega-3 fats matter?

Three graduate projects examine how the molecular form of omega-3 fats shapes their handling in the body.

Metabolic syndromeTissue incorporationMeal-by-meal handlingThe bigger picture

Fish oil supplements are one of the most common ways people boost their intake of omega-3 fats — EPA and DHA — which support heart health, brain function, and inflammation control. But not all omega-3 supplements deliver these fats in the same molecular package. Three graduate theses from Dr. Scott Harding's lab at Memorial University asked a related question from different angles: does the form omega-3s come in change how well the body uses them?

1. Fish Oil and Phospholipids in a Model of Metabolic Syndrome

Jagatheesan Manoharan, MSc, 2022

The modern Western diet — high in calories, saturated fat, sugar, and salt, with too much omega-6 relative to omega-3 — is a major driver of metabolic syndrome: the cluster of obesity, insulin resistance, high blood fat, and high blood pressure that raises the risk of diabetes and heart disease.

This study tested whether adding fish oil, with or without a phospholipid (soy lecithin), could protect against metabolic syndrome in Yucatan miniature pigs fed a Western-style diet. Pigs were split into three groups: Western diet alone, Western diet plus fish oil, and Western diet plus fish oil and phospholipid.

What they found: After six weeks, blood glucose handling differed significantly between groups, and there was a trend toward higher cholesterol in the fish-oil group compared to the other two. However, COVID-19 lab shutdowns prevented the team from completing the fatty acid tissue analysis that was meant to link these outcomes back to how much omega-3 was actually absorbed. As a result, the study couldn't reach a firm conclusion on whether fish oil (with or without phospholipid) protects against metabolic syndrome — that link remains an open question for future work.

2. Phospholipids and Fish Oil Bioavailability

Efe Obade Princess, MSc, 2024

This study picked up a related thread: does adding a phospholipid (phosphatidylcholine) to a fish oil–containing diet help omega-3 fats reach the tissues where they're needed most — like the brain, heart, and retina?

Yucatan miniature pigs were fed one of three diets for four months: a Western diet, a Western diet plus fish oil, or a Western diet plus fish oil and phospholipid. Researchers then measured how much omega-3 accumulated in different organs, and checked markers of inflammation in the blood.

What they found: Fish oil clearly raised omega-3 levels compared to no fish oil at all. Adding phospholipid pushed those omega-3s further into specific tissues — increasing incorporation in the brain and heart, and in the brain and retina specifically compared to fish oil alone. Inflammation markers were more mixed: one marker (TNFα) was unexpectedly higher in the phospholipid group, while another (IL-6) didn't differ across groups. The overall picture suggests that adding phospholipids can influence tissue-specific omega-3 incorporation. Tissue measurements alone do not establish greater total intestinal absorption or a health benefit.

3. How Meal Structure Shapes Omega-3 Absorption, Meal by Meal

Emma Burnett, MSc, 2026

While the previous two studies looked at long-term feeding, this study zoomed into the hours right after a single meal — when omega-3s first enter the bloodstream — to see whether the molecular structure of the oil changes that early handling.

Growing piglets were fed test meals containing either standard fish oil (which carries omega-3s mainly as triglycerides), krill oil (which carries them mainly as phospholipids), fish oil combined with an added phospholipid, or control fats. Blood was sampled repeatedly over eight hours to track fat and omega-3 levels.

What they found: All the marine oils raised blood omega-3 levels, and EPA rose faster and more reliably than DHA across the board. But the oils didn't behave identically: krill oil steered omega-3s into different carrier molecules in the blood than fish oil did, and adding phospholipid to fish oil shifted where its EPA ended up too. Despite these different routing patterns, the total amount of omega-3 absorbed was similar across all the marine oil sources. In other words, oil structure changes where omega-3s go once absorbed, even when it doesn't change how much gets absorbed overall.

The Bigger Picture

Together, these three studies build a progressively more detailed picture of the same question: it's not just how much omega-3 you take in — it's the molecular form it's delivered in that shapes where it ends up in the body. The tissue-feeding study suggests that adding phospholipid to fish oil can influence omega-3 incorporation in the brain, heart, and retina. The separate meal study found differences in circulating lipid carriers across oil sources; those blood measurements do not by themselves establish tissue targeting or total intestinal absorption. What remains less certain is whether this improved tissue targeting translates into better protection against conditions like metabolic syndrome — a question the Manoharan thesis attempted to answer but couldn't fully resolve due to pandemic-related lab disruptions, leaving it open for future research.

All three studies were conducted in the Department of Biochemistry at Memorial University, supervised or co-supervised by Dr. Scott Harding, using pig models chosen for their similarity to human digestion.
