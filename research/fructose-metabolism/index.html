FRUCTOSE METABOLISM

How phenotypes impact metabolite concentrations.

Research story by Laura Grasmeyer

This project account preserves the background, study objectives and preliminary progress reported on the lab’s original research page.

BackgroundObjectivesProgressWhy it mattersReferences

Background

Fructose is a simple sugar or monosaccharide found naturally in foods such as apples, pears, and honey, or as an additive in foods such as soda, candy, and other processed foods. As an additive, fructose is introduced into foods in forms such as sucrose or high-fructose corn syrup (HFCS). Sucrose is a disaccharide composed of glucose and fructose chemically bonded together, while HFCS contains approximately equal parts of free glucose and fructose, which are separate monosaccharides. Glucose, in particular, plays a critical role in meeting the body’s energy requirements. Fructose, on the other hand, can contribute to energy demands, however, it must first be converted to different metabolites. An understanding of fructose metabolism under physiological conditions that are abnormal is important as most of our understanding of fructose metabolism comes from studies conducted using low intakes in healthy animals. Currently, the food industry uses fructose in very high amounts and is exposing humans and animals (companion and agricultural) without an understanding of how this monosaccharide is metabolized in unhealthy organisms or in situations of energy excess.

Once consumed, fructose has different metabolic fates in the body. It was originally thought that all fructose is processed by the liver where it was converted to different metabolites. However, recent studies have indicated that the small intestine plays an important role in fructose metabolism and handling. In the small intestine, fructose is absorbed into enterocytes, the cells that line the small intestine, by the GLUT5 transporter (Barone et al., 2009). Once absorbed, fructose is converted to fructose-1-phosphate by keto hexokinase, then subsequently converted to 3-carbon intermediates glyceraldehyde and dihydroxyacetone phosphate. These 3 carbon intermediates can then be combined with additional carbons to form glucose which can re-enter circulation and enter various glucose-related metabolic pathways (Jang et al., 2018). While this system acts to shield the liver from fructose, the small intestine can become overwhelmed with larger doses of fructose, causing spillover to the liver.

Fructose that reaches the liver, similarly to its metabolism in the small intestine, is converted to fructose-1-phosphate by the fructokinase enzyme, then cleaved by aldolase B to form glyceraldehyde (which is quickly converted to glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate) and dihydroxyacetone phosphate. Both glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate and dihydroxyacetone phosphate are intermediates of the glycolysis pathway. The fate of fructose-derived carbons after this breakdown is controversial, and the literature is often contradictory. Some sources claim that fructose-to-glucose conversion occurs in the liver, however, these were published before the discovery of fructose-to-glucose conversion in the small intestine. Therefore, results indicating increased glucose in circulation after oral ingestion of fructose may reflect conversion to glucose in the small intestine rather than the liver (Tran et al., 2010). An alternative fate of the fructose-derived 3-carbon intermediates in the liver is glycolysis, which they enter after the initial regulatory steps of glycolysis before glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate and dihydroxyacetone phosphate are synthesized within the glycolysis pathway. Ultimately, glycolysis will convert the 3-carbon intermediates to pyruvate and, subsequently, acetyl-coA. Under normal conditions, acetyl-CoA can enter the Krebs cycle for energy production, however, when in excess, acetyl-CoA can contribute to endogenous fatty acid synthesis (Softic et al., 2016).

Another alternative fate of ingested fructose arises when a dose of fructose is incompletely absorbed into the small intestine. Rather than entering the enterocyte, unabsorbed fructose remains in the lumen of the small intestine, typically occurring after large doses of fructose or in cases of fructose intolerance. Regardless, fructose remaining in the lumen can be fermented by gut-fermenting bacteria, converting it into short-chain fatty acids (SCFA). SCFAs commonly found in humans include acetate, propionate, isobutyrate, butyrate, isovalerate, and valerate, however, the major products of carbohydrate fermentation are acetate, propionate, and butyrate (Zhang et al., 2023).

A more unique potential fate of fructose concerns the fructose converted to glucose that enters glucose-related metabolic pathways. One such pathway is the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP), which converts glucose-6-phosphate from glycolysis into ribose-5-phosphate sugars while also generating nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH). Within the interconversions of sugars in the non-oxidative stage of the PPP, different byproducts are synthesized, one such leading to the synthesis of a sugar alcohol called erythritol (Hootman et al., 2017). The canonical synthesis of erythritol is from glucose via the PPP, however, erythritol synthesis from fructose has yet to be demonstrated in animals. This may be possible through the conversion of fructose to glucose, which can then enter the PPP. Additionally, erythritol has been a molecule of interest as it has shown promise as a predictive biomarker of weight gain as well as metabolic diseases such as coronary heart disease and type II diabetes (Wang et al., 2019; Rebholz et al., 2018).

Since metabolism is dynamic, it can be influenced by different factors. These factors, such as dietary components, exposure to exercise, or sex, can attribute to different phenotypes an individual may possess, and in turn, may alter how nutrients are handled by the organism. For example, our lab has previously found that prior exposure to fructose consumption can increase fructose clearance by converting it to glucose. Furthermore, this effect is seen to a greater extent in female mice (Walsh, 2024). Therefore, this project will address how different phenotypes metabolize fructose and determine the fate of various fructose metabolites.

Objectives

The project aims to study fructose metabolism across phenotypes by examining how dietary fructose, exercise, and sex influence its metabolic pathways.

The objectives of this project are:

1. Develop mass spectrometry methodologies to measure fructose, glucose, SCFAs, and erythritol, including isotopically enriched version of these metabolites.

2. Compare metabolite concentrations across groups differing in diet, exercise, and sex to assess their impact on their major and minor metabolic pathways.

3. Trace stably-labeled carbons from 13C-fructose in these metabolites to identify how the fructose-derived metabolic pathways vary with phenotype.

The first sub-objective for this project is to determine a sample preparation and derivatization method for the simultaneous determination of the following metabolites: glucose, fructose, SCFAs, and erythritol. This method will ultimately be used on plasma samples and metabolite concentrations will be measured via GC-MS. In GC-MS measurements, derivatization is a crucial step for increasing the sensitivity of compounds to the GC-MS. Common derivatization agents for GC-MS include silylation agents which replace active groups with trimethyl-silyl groups, or acylation agents which replace these groups with trifluoroacetamide groups. Previously, silylation agents such as N,O- bis(trimethylsilyl)trifluoroacetamide (BSTFA) or n-methyl-n-trimethylsilylfluoracetemide (MSTFA) have been used for the derivatization of sugars such as glucose and fructose, and sugar alcohols such as erythritol (Ortiz and Field, 2021). Therefore, I hypothesize that a silylation agent will be the best derivatization method for the simultaneous determination of glucose, fructose, SCFAs, and erythritol.

Once a method for GC-MS determination of glucose, fructose, SCFAs, and erythritol has been determined, our next objective is to use this method to measure the concentrations of these metabolites across male and female obese individuals with different levels of fructose consumption and availability to exercise. With this, we can investigate the effect of these factors on glucose, fructose, SCFA, and erythritol outputs to gain insights into how fructose metabolism is altered under the influence of these factors. I hypothesize that increased fructose from the diet will result in increased levels of glucose, SCFA, and erythritol, as excess fructose will be converted to these metabolites. I also hypothesize that exercise will have a decreasing effect on these metabolites. To approach this objective, male and female mice were provided with a high-energy and high-saturated fat diet containing either 0% calories from fructose, or a diet containing 20% calories from fructose to investigate the influence of dietary fructose on metabolites. All diets were isocaloric for carbohydrate content. In order to investigate the influence of exercise, mice were either provided with a running wheel to increase activity or were not provided with a running wheel to limit options for activity. After 24 weeks of interventions, plasma samples were collected and are to be analyzed via GC-MS to compare the concentrations of glucose, fructose, SCFA, and erythritol across the different factored groups. With this, we expect to see elevated concentrations of glucose, SCFA, and erythritol in the 20% calories from fructose diet group compared to the 0% calories from fructose group. Likewise, I expect to see elevated concentrations of these metabolites in the non-exercise group when compared to the exercise group.

The final objective of this project is to investigate the possible fates of fructose-derived carbons in our metabolites of interest. It has been documented that fructose carbons have been found in circulating glucose molecules, indicating that fructose can be metabolized into glucose. Therefore, to elucidate further fates of fructose carbons, this project aims to identify fructose-derived labelled carbons in glucose, SCFA, and erythritol. I hypothesize that we will identify labelled carbons in each metabolite. To approach this objective, mice were administered an oral gavage containing U-13C-labelled fructose 30 minutes before their necropsies. In these 30 minutes the fructose has time to be metabolized into different products, potentially our metabolites of interest. To identify these labelled carbons in our metabolites of interest, the GC-MS spectra from plasma samples will be investigated for isotope signals from metabolites, which indicate labelled carbons from fructose. This will allow us to obtain more information on the metabolic fates of fructose and the metabolic pathways fructose is processed by.

Preliminary progress

So far, this project has investigated the concentrations of SCFAs in fecal matter from the caecum across different groups categorized by fructose consumption, exercise, and sex. This is significant progress towards my second objective, which is to compare the concentrations of metabolites across the groups. Specifically, this helps provide insights into the fate of fructose in SCFAs, as fructose unabsorbed by the small intestine can be fermented by gut fermenting bacteria, producing SCFAs that reside in the caecum. Currently, no significant differences in the concentrations of acetate, propionate, butyrate, isobutyrate, valerate, and isovalerate across the groups have been observed. These preliminary comparisons did not detect significant differences in caecal SCFA concentrations; they do not establish that SCFA production is unaffected.

In addition to the analysis of the concentrations of fecal SCFAs, the concentrations of erythritol found in plasma have been identified in the 20% calories from fructose without exercise groups. When comparing erythritol concentrations in the male versus the female mice in these groups, no significant differences were detected. This preliminary comparison does not establish that erythritol synthesis is unaffected by sex.

Why this work matters

This project will investigate fructose metabolism as it changes under the influence of different factors of dietary fructose, exposure to exercise, and sex. These factors are susceptible to altering the phenotype of an individual, such as their metabolism of fructose and how it is handled in one’s body. Understanding how different phenotypes metabolize fructose is key to uncovering which phenotypes process it more efficiently and which may exhibit altered metabolic pathways. This research can also reveal how fructose enters and impacts metabolic pathways, potentially identifying markers like fructose-derived metabolites that accumulate with excessive intake. Furthermore, this work contributes to our fundamental understanding of metabolic pathways, highlighting how they adapt to variations in phenotype. Such insights are critical to advancing knowledge of canonical metabolic processes and their flexibility under differing conditions.
