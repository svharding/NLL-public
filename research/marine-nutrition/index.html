MARINE NUTRITION

Aquaculture & bioactives

Exploring green sea urchins, local kelp and the nutritional potential of marine by-products.

Our Marine Nutrition research looks at underused resources from Atlantic Canada’s oceans and asks how they might add value to human and animal food systems. One major focus is the green sea urchin (Strongylocentrotus droebachiensis), a species that is abundant along our coasts but remains largely untapped as an aquaculture product. PhD student Madeline Borland is leading this work, which spans everything from waste-stream biochemistry to live-animal feeding and lighting trials.

Finding value in shells and spines

Sea urchins are farmed primarily for their roe (uni), a delicacy especially prized in Japan. But roe makes up only a small fraction of the animal’s total mass—up to 85% of the harvested biomass, mostly shell and spine, is currently discarded. Maddie’s research asks whether this “waste” actually holds untapped value, and whether we can grow better urchins, more reliably, using indoor aquaculture.

The first part of her work characterizes what’s actually in these shells and spines. Green sea urchin shells contain a family of pigments called naphthoquinones, which have shown antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and antimicrobial properties in research on other species. Maddie compared shells from urchins harvested at two different Newfoundland sites (Holyrood and St. Philip’s) to see how much their fatty acid and pigment content varies simply based on where they’re collected. Understanding this natural variability is a critical first step before anyone can reliably market these by-products.

How light shapes aquaculture

The second, and largest, part of her project moves from wild-harvested animals into a controlled aquaculture setting. Working with the Ocean Sciences Centre, Maddie ran an 8-week lighting trial with 120 live sea urchins, testing four light regimes—0, 6, 12, and 24 hours of light per day—to see how photoperiod affects shell, spine, and gonad development.

Light exposure is an underexplored influence on urchin physiology, yet this is exactly the kind of variable that indoor aquaculture facilities need to control. Her hypothesis is that constant light exposure may hurt feeding and gonad growth compared to no light at all. If supported, that would be valuable, practical information for anyone trying to farm this species indoors: less artificial lighting could save energy and cost. This remains a hypothesis, rather than a reported outcome of the trial.

Local kelp and pigment bioactivity

The final piece of the project looks at feeding urchins kelp-based gelatine blocks made from locally harvested kelp, tracking how this diet influences the same lipid and pigment profiles, and testing whether the extracted pigments have antioxidant capacity in liver cell models.

A marine opportunity for Atlantic Canada

Together, these studies aim to answer a practical question with real economic potential for the province: can Newfoundland turn an underused marine species, and the waste it currently generates, into a viable aquaculture product?

By working out the basic biology of how diet, light, and location shape sea urchin composition, this project is laying the scientific groundwork needed before echinoculture (sea urchin farming) can become commercially viable in Atlantic Canada—turning a fishery by-product problem into a potential nutraceutical and food-industry opportunity.
