LIFESTYLE & HEALTH

Sleep, activity & food choices

How everyday routines shape diet and energy balance in children and shift workers.

Children’s sleep and food choicesShift work and the SWEAT StudyConnecting daily routines with health

Our Lifestyle & Health research asks a simple question: how do the ordinary rhythms of daily life—sleep, activity, screen time, and work schedules—shape what we eat and how our bodies balance energy? Two recent Master’s projects from the lab tackle this question in different age groups and life circumstances, and together they show how much these everyday factors matter for diet and health.

Children’s sleep and food choices

Roya Shamsi · Master’s project

The first project, led by Roya Shamsi, looked at children aged 9 to 12. Childhood obesity is a growing concern, and while diet and physical activity get most of the attention, sleep is often overlooked. Shamsi’s pilot study recruited 22 children and used a mix of actigraphy (wearable sleep and activity trackers) and detailed 24-hour dietary recalls to capture a full picture of each child’s day.

Over half the children slept less than the Canadian recommendation, most had more than two hours of screen time daily, and the majority fell short on fruit and vegetable intake while exceeding recommended added sugar. The study also found links between sleep patterns and food choices, suggesting that better sleep could be a useful, low-cost target to investigate for childhood obesity prevention. These pilot associations do not establish that sleep caused the dietary differences.

Just as importantly, this pilot demonstrated that combining objective sleep and activity tracking with dietary assessment is practical in a pediatric population, laying the groundwork for larger studies.

Shift work and the SWEAT Study

Varleen Kaur · Master’s project

The second project, led by Varleen Kaur, turned to an adult population that is often overlooked in nutrition research: shift workers. Newfoundland and Labrador has a large shift-working population, yet we know surprisingly little about how non-standard work schedules affect diet and lifestyle here.

The SWEAT Study (Sleep, Work, Energy intake, and Activity) compared day workers and shift workers using wearable activity monitors, dietary recalls, and validated questionnaires on sleep quality, physical activity, chronotype, and stress. The study successfully recruited and retained a full pilot cohort, confirming the feasibility of a larger study.

Shift workers reported diet quality scores that were slightly higher than day workers, and their work- and transport-related physical activity was greater—though these differences didn’t always match what the wearable trackers picked up, highlighting how self-reported and objectively measured behaviour can tell different stories. The findings point to physical activity as a promising target for future interventions aimed at protecting the health of shift workers.

Connecting daily routines with health

Together, these two studies illustrate the throughline of our Lifestyle & Health research: energy balance isn’t just about calories in and calories out. It’s shaped by when we sleep, how much we move, how we spend our screen time, and the schedules imposed by modern work and family life.

By pairing objective measurement tools with careful dietary assessment, our trainees are building the evidence base needed to design realistic, effective interventions—whether that means helping children sleep better or supporting shift workers in maintaining healthy habits despite disrupted routines. Both projects were completed as pilot and feasibility studies, and both are now informing the design of larger follow-up research in our lab.
